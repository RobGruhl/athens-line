Welcome aboard. You're starting in Syntagma, which means constitution, and the square earned that name in a single night. A full loop takes about an hour and twenty minutes if you stay in your seat, and you can hop off and back on wherever you like, so there's no need to see everything at once.

The long, plain building across the avenue was built as a palace for Otto, a Bavarian prince whom the great powers made king of the new Greek state in eighteen thirty-two. He arrived the following year, aged seventeen, with Bavarian advisers and Bavarian soldiers, and his father, King Ludwig, lent him the money for the palace. The men who had actually fought the War of Independence watched foreigners take the best jobs while the king ruled without a constitution, and after ten years their patience ran out.

On the night of the second of September, eighteen forty-three, the Athens garrison marched into this square, led by a cavalry colonel named Dimitrios Kallergis. Behind the plot was a group of veterans and politicians that included General Makrygiannis, whose neighbourhood is our next stop. By morning half the town had joined them. Otto came to the window and was told, very politely and with a great many bayonets, that nobody was leaving until he granted a constitution. By the afternoon he had agreed. An assembly wrote it the following spring, and the square has been Syntagma ever since. Otto kept his throne for another nineteen years, until the Greeks ran out of patience again and sent him home to Bavaria.

The palace has housed the Parliament since the nineteen thirties. In front of it, look for the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, a dying warrior carved in relief, with a line from Pericles beside him: the whole earth is the tomb of famous men. The guards are Evzones, in a uniform taken from the mountain fighters of the War of Independence. The kilt is said to have four hundred pleats, one for each year of Ottoman rule, and each red clog weighs about a kilo and a half, with dozens of nails in the sole, which is why that slow, high-kicking march sounds the way it does. They change every hour, and the full ceremony with the band is at eleven on Sunday mornings.

The grand hotel on the corner of the square is the Grande Bretagne, a hotel since eighteen seventy-four. On Christmas Day nineteen forty-four, when it was the British headquarters, resistance fighters packed the sewers beneath it with dynamite, then called the attack off when they learned that Winston Churchill had just arrived. History in Athens is like that. It's usually right under your feet.

This neighbourhood is named after General Ioannis Makrygiannis, who owned a house and fields here, just under the south side of the Acropolis, and he's one of the best people you can meet in Greek history. The stop itself is named for Melina Merkouri, the film star who became Minister of Culture and spent years fighting to bring the Parthenon sculptures home. You'll hear more about her at the next stop.

He was born in the mountains of central Greece in seventeen ninety-seven, had almost no schooling, and was a trader and a fighter by his twenties. In the War of Independence he was wounded again and again, three times in the head and neck while helping to hold the Acropolis during the Ottoman siege of eighteen twenty-six. Then, in eighteen twenty-nine, stationed in Argos, a man who had only just learned to write sat down and began his memoirs, in the spoken Greek of the villages, spelled the way it sounded.

The memoirs are furious, funny and honest. He's hard on the politicians, hard on the foreigners, and harder still on his fellow captains when they put themselves first. His best-known line says that the country was won by everyone together, that we stand in the we and not in the I. He was one of the leaders of the night you just heard about in Syntagma, and in eighteen fifty-three a government that found him inconvenient put him on trial and sentenced him to death. The sentence was commuted, and he was released the following year, but when he died in eighteen sixty-four the manuscript was hidden away. It was found decades later with the help of his son and published in nineteen-oh-seven, and George Seferis, who later won the Nobel Prize, called him one of the greatest masters of modern Greek prose.

Up the slope is Plaka, the oldest part of the modern town, with its lanes, tavernas and neoclassical houses. Climb high enough and you reach Anafiotika, a tiny village of whitewashed cube houses jammed against the foot of the rock. It was built from the eighteen forties by builders from the island of Anafi, who came to work on Otto's palace. The story goes that they made use of an old Ottoman rule that a house put up between sunset and sunrise belonged to whoever built it, so they built their homes by night. Whether or not the law said quite that, the houses are still there, and so are the cats.

The glass and concrete building is the Acropolis Museum, designed by the Swiss-American architect Bernard Tschumi and opened in June two thousand and nine. When the builders dug the foundations they found a whole Athenian neighbourhood underneath, with streets, houses, workshops and drains spanning many centuries, so the museum stands on columns above it. As you walk in, you look down through the glass floor at the city it replaced.

The museum was built to make an argument, and the top floor is where it makes it. The Parthenon Gallery is turned, by about twenty-three degrees, to line up exactly with the temple up on the rock, which you can see through the glass walls. Around it hangs the frieze, the long band of carving that once ran around the top of the temple's inner walls, behind the columns. It shows the great procession of the Panathenaic festival: horsemen, chariots, musicians, cattle led to sacrifice, and the gods waiting at the end. Here it's hung at eye level, in its original order. The pieces still in Athens are honey-coloured marble. The pieces in the British Museum are shown as plaster casts, bright white, and there are a great many of them.

They went to London in the early eighteen hundreds, when Lord Elgin, the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, had his agents remove about half of the surviving sculpture from the temple. He sold it to the British government in eighteen sixteen. Greece has pressed for their return for generations, and hardest since the actress Melina Merkouri, as Minister of Culture, made it a national cause in the nineteen eighties. The British Museum's answer is that the marbles were acquired legally and belong in a museum of world culture. This building is the reply, with the frieze laid out in the same shape as the temple itself and room left for the rest.

On the floor below are the Caryatids, five of the six marble women who held up the porch of the Erechtheion. They were brought indoors to protect them from the city's pollution, and cleaned with lasers in front of the visitors. The sixth is in London too, and the place where she belongs is left empty.

If you have time, take a coffee on the restaurant terrace. It looks straight up at the rock, which is the whole point of the place.

This is the stop for the Acropolis itself, and for the hills around it, which are some of the best walking in the city.

Up on the rock, the Parthenon looks like a building made of straight lines, and almost none of them are. It went up between four forty-seven and four thirty-two BC, under Pericles, with the architects Iktinos and Kallikrates and the sculptor Pheidias in charge of the carving. The platform rises gently toward the middle, by about six centimetres across the ends and eleven along the sides, so it's really a very shallow dome. The columns swell slightly partway up, and they all lean a little inward; carry them upward and they'd meet a couple of kilometres up in the sky. The corner columns are thicker than the rest, because a column seen against open sky looks thinner than it is. Nearly every block was cut for one place and no other, which is a headache for the restorers and the reason it looks, from below, effortlessly straight.

It survived more or less whole for two thousand years, as a temple, then a church, then a mosque. Then in September sixteen eighty-seven a Venetian army besieged the Ottoman garrison, who had stored their gunpowder inside, and a Venetian mortar shell found it. The explosion blew out the middle of the building, and the restorers have been piecing it back together ever since.

Now look for the low, rocky hill facing the Acropolis from the west. That's the Pnyx, and it matters as much as the temple. This is where the assembly of Athens met, every adult male citizen who chose to come, often six thousand at a time, sitting on the slope to debate war and peace and vote by a show of hands. Anyone could speak from the stone platform, which is still there, cut into the rock. Attendance was a problem, naturally. Aristophanes jokes about the rope dipped in red paint that was swept through the market to herd people up the hill, so that anyone with red on his clothes was known to be dodging. Eventually Athens simply started paying people to come.

On the way up you pass the Odeon of Herodes Atticus, the steep stone theatre built in the second century AD by a fabulously rich Athenian in memory of his wife, and still used for concerts on summer nights. Between the rock and the Pnyx is the Areopagus, the bare outcrop where St Paul is said to have preached. And the stone paths you walk on up here were laid in the nineteen fifties by the architect Dimitris Pikionis, every piece chosen and set by hand, in the spirit of the temple above.

The tall columns in the field ahead belong to the Temple of Olympian Zeus, which has a fair claim to be the slowest building project in the history of Athens.

It was begun around five twenty BC by Hippias and Hipparchos, the sons of the tyrant Peisistratos, who wanted a temple bigger than anything the Greeks had built. Then the tyranny fell, and the democracy that replaced it had no interest in finishing a tyrant's vanity project. Aristotle later used it as his example of how tyrants keep their people too busy and too poor to rebel. For more than three centuries it stood half-built.

In one seventy-four BC a Hellenistic king, Antiochus the Fourth, decided to finish it, and hired a Roman architect, Decimus Cossutius, who redesigned it with a hundred and four tall Corinthian columns. Antiochus died, and work stopped again. Then the Roman general Sulla sacked Athens in eighty-six BC and shipped some of the unfinished columns off to Rome for a temple on the Capitol, which is a very Roman way of helping.

The man who finally finished it, around one thirty-two AD, was the emperor Hadrian, who loved Athens, had served as one of its chief magistrates before he became emperor, and wanted the city to love him back. Inside he placed a gold and ivory statue of Zeus, and beside it, to be on the safe side, a colossal statue of himself. Fifteen of the columns still stand, more than seventeen metres tall. A sixteenth blew down in a storm in eighteen fifty-two and lies where it fell, its drums spread out like a toppled stack of coins.

The gateway beside the road is Hadrian's Arch. On the side facing the Acropolis the inscription says, This is Athens, the ancient city of Theseus. On the side facing the temple it says, This is the city of Hadrian, and not of Theseus. Historians used to think the arch marked the line between the old town and Hadrian's new quarter, but excavation has shown that the old city wall ran somewhere else. So it's less a boundary than a boast, carved in marble by a man who had paid for a great deal of the city and wanted everyone to know it.

The green wall along the avenue is the National Garden, and for its first eighty years or so it was nobody's garden but the queen's. The bus passes this stop twice, once now and again after the stadium on the way back toward Syntagma, so if you don't hop off this time, you'll get a second chance.

Queen Amalia, Otto's wife, had it laid out from eighteen thirty-eight behind the new palace. Her German agronomist, Friedrich Schmidt, imported some five hundred species of plants, along with peacocks, ducks and turtles, and a good many of the plants did what northern plants do in an Athenian summer, which is die. What survived became a shady maze of paths, ponds and tall trees, a few degrees cooler than the street. It opened to the public in nineteen twenty-three as the National Garden. The ducks and turtles are still there, and it's still the best place in central Athens to sit in the shade without buying anything.

On its far side is the Zappeion, a neoclassical hall with a curved, colonnaded courtyard, and its story starts with a man named Evangelos Zappas. He fought in the War of Independence, made a fortune in what is now Romania, and spent it on an idea a generation ahead of its time: reviving the Olympic Games. He paid for Olympic contests in Athens in eighteen fifty-nine, and his will funded more, decades before the modern Games began. It also paid for this hall, designed by Theophil Hansen, the Danish architect you'll meet again on this route, and opened in eighteen eighty-eight. When the first modern Olympics came in eighteen ninety-six, the fencing was held here.

Zappas died in Romania in eighteen sixty-five. His body was eventually buried in his home village, but his head was brought here and sealed into a wall of the building he paid for, which is a level of commitment most donors stop short of.

The Zappeion has kept its place in the national story. In May nineteen seventy-nine Greece signed its treaty to join the European Community here, and for the Athens Olympics of two thousand and four it was the press centre. Just beyond the garden, across the road, is the marble stadium, which you'll reach after the museums.

This stretch of Vasilissis Sofias is the city's museum mile, and four museums are within a few minutes' walk of this stop.

The Benaki Museum, in the family mansion of Antonis Benakis, opened in nineteen thirty and covers Greek art and life from prehistory to the twentieth century, including two early paintings by El Greco, who started out as an icon painter on Crete. The Museum of Cycladic Art holds the Goulandris family's collection of marble figures from the Cycladic islands, made around five thousand years ago: folded arms, tilted heads, and faces with nothing but a nose. They look startlingly modern, and the influence runs backwards; artists like Brancusi and Modigliani looked hard at figures like these. The War Museum, opened in nineteen seventy-five in a stark modernist block, runs from ancient armour to the Second World War, with fighter planes and field guns parked outside.

The fourth, the Byzantine and Christian Museum, sits in the Villa Ilissia, built in the eighteen forties for Sophie de Marbois, the Duchess of Plaisance. She was born in Philadelphia, the daughter of a French diplomat, married into the Napoleonic nobility, and came to the new Greece with a great deal of money and very definite opinions. She bought land along the river Ilissos, built herself a villa, and was remembered by a neighbour as dressing all in white and going about with six big white dogs.

The sad heart of her story is her daughter Elisa, who died of pneumonia on a journey to Beirut in the eighteen thirties. The Duchess had her embalmed, brought her back to Athens and kept her in a crypt beneath her house, for years. When the Duchess died in eighteen fifty-four, she was buried with her daughter at her castle on the slopes of Mount Penteli. A metro station on the airport line, Doukissis Plakentias, still carries her name.

Uphill from the avenue is Kolonaki, named after a small ancient column in its square. It's the smart part of town, and its cafés are where Athens comes to be seen nursing a freddo espresso, the iced coffee that Greeks drink for most of the year, slowly, for an hour or more. Above it rises Lycabettus, the tallest hill in the centre, with a white chapel on the summit and a funicular railway for anyone who'd rather not climb.

The long white slab of a hotel is the old Athens Hilton, and when it opened in nineteen sixty-three it was one of the most modern buildings in Greece and the subject of a great deal of argument. Four Greek architects gave the city a big slice of international modernism at a time when Athens was still mostly low-rise, and critics complained that it was far too big for its surroundings. Its defenders pointed to the ends of the building, covered in huge incised marble figures designed by the painter Yannis Moralis, a very Greek signature on a very American hotel. For half a century it was where Athens went for weddings, conferences and cocktails on the roof. It closed in twenty twenty-two for a long rebuild, and reopened in April twenty twenty-six as the Conrad Athens, The Ilisian, with the Moralis marbles kept. The other big hotel by this stop is the Caravel.

Across the avenue is the National Gallery, the country's main collection of Greek painting. It closed for years for a major expansion and reopened in March twenty twenty-one, a day before the two hundredth anniversary of the start of the War of Independence.

It also has the best crime story on this route. In nineteen forty-nine Picasso gave the Greek people a painting of a woman's head, inscribed on the back as a tribute to them, in honour of their resistance in the Second World War. In January twenty twelve, a thief set off the gallery's alarms several times to distract the guard, then came in through a balcony door and took the Picasso, a Mondrian and a small drawing, in a matter of minutes. Nothing was heard of them for nine years. Then, in June twenty twenty-one, police arrested a construction worker and amateur painter, a Picasso enthusiast, who led them to a gorge some forty-five kilometres outside Athens where the paintings were hidden. He told them he'd used the drawing to wipe blood from a cut on his hand and then flushed it down the toilet. It has never been found.

And by Rizari Park lies something far older. In nineteen ninety-six, digging foundations for a new museum of modern art, archaeologists hit the remains of a gymnasium and a wrestling ground. It was the Lyceum, where Aristotle set up his school in three thirty-five BC, in a sanctuary of Apollo. He liked to teach while walking up and down the covered paths, so his followers became known as the Peripatetics, the people who walk about. Logic, biology, ethics, even a project to collect and compare the constitutions of more than a hundred and fifty Greek cities: a surprising amount of the way we still sort knowledge started on these paths. Sulla, our Roman friend from the Temple of Zeus, wrecked the place in eighty-six BC. The art museum went elsewhere, and the foundations are now a small archaeological park, where you can walk up and down exactly as Aristotle did.

This is the Kallimarmaro, the beautiful marble, and it's the only stadium in the world built entirely of marble. Look at the shape: a long, narrow horseshoe, closed at one end, built for running up and down rather than round and round, with bends so tight that a modern sprinter would hate them.

The first stadium here was built by the statesman Lycurgus around three thirty BC, for the games of the Panathenaic festival, with the crowd sitting on the slopes of this little valley. Around one forty AD Herodes Atticus, a fabulously rich Athenian who was also a Roman senator, rebuilt the whole thing in marble from Mount Penteli. He's the same Herodes who built the Odeon under the Acropolis. Over the following centuries the marble was carted off for lime and building stone, and by the eighteen hundreds there was little left but a grassy hollow.

Then came the revival of the Olympics. The site was excavated around eighteen seventy, and for eighteen ninety-six a merchant from Alexandria, George Averoff, paid for the stadium to be rebuilt in fresh Pentelic marble. His statue stands by the entrance. On the sixth of April eighteen ninety-six the first modern Olympic Games opened here, in front of tens of thousands of people.

The race everyone remembers is the marathon, run for the first time from Marathon to this stadium, and the Greeks badly wanted to win it. A Frenchman led for a long way, then an Australian, who collapsed. The winner, in just under three hours, was Spyridon Louis, a water carrier from the village of Marousi, who had stopped on the way at an inn in Pikermi for a glass of wine, or a little cognac, depending on which family story you believe. As he came into the stadium, the Crown Prince and his brother jumped down and ran the last stretch beside him. Omonia means concord, or harmony, which is a hopeful name for one of the busiest squares in Athens. It's a crossroads of avenues, metro lines and buses, and for well over a century it's been where newcomers first met the city. People from villages all over Greece stepped off here looking for work. Later came people from Albania and Africa, Pakistan and Bangladesh and the Middle East, and the streets around still hum with their shops, their food and their languages.

The largest arrival of all came after nineteen twenty-two. When the war in Asia Minor ended in defeat, Greeks whose families had lived for generations along the Turkish coast and inland, in cities like Smyrna, had to leave, and the following year a treaty made the exchange of populations official. Around one point two million refugees came to a country of about five million people, most of them women, children and the elderly. Athens took them in wherever it could, in schools, churches and warehouses, and even in the boxes of the old Municipal Theatre, a few streets from here.

Over the following years, they built whole new neighbourhoods on the edges of the city and named them after the places they had lost: Nea Smyrni, New Smyrna, and Nea Ionia, and Nea Filadelfeia. They brought their trades and their spices, dishes like the cumin-scented meatballs called soutzoukakia, and the music that became rebetiko, the Greek blues, sung in small cafés late at night. Within a generation they were simply part of what it means to be Athenian, and a great many families here have a grandmother from Asia Minor, and her recipes.

On the corners, look for the kiosks, the periptera. The first appeared around nineteen eleven, and before long licences to run them were given to men wounded in the wars, in place of a pension. A widow or a child could inherit the licence. Generations of kiosk keepers have sat inside those tiny boxes from early morning till late at night, selling newspapers and cigarettes, sweets and bus tickets, cold water and aspirin, with directions thrown in for free. There are fewer of them now, but they're still the city's corner shop and, for many older Athenians, the place for a daily chat.

In the older cafés around the square, you'll still find men lingering over a tiny cup of Greek coffee, boiled in a small long-handled pot and drunk slowly, leaving the grounds at the bottom of the cup. Some people still turn the cup over and read their fortune in them.

This square is named for a hero of the War of Independence, Georgios Karaiskakis, but the neighbourhood around it takes its name from something much more domestic: silk. Metaxourgeio means the silk works. In the eighteen fifties a company turned a large building here into a silk mill, and at its busiest it employed more than four hundred workers, most of them women and girls, reeling and twisting the fine thread. For a time it was the largest factory of its kind in Greece. In the eighteen seventies cheaper silk from China arrived and the mill closed, but the name stayed. The building has been restored, and now it's the city's Municipal Gallery.

Just to the south is one of the quietest places in central Athens, the archaeological site of Kerameikos. It was the potters' quarter of the ancient city, and it gave us our word ceramic. The potters came for the clay along the banks of the Eridanos, a little stream that still trickles through the site, and for centuries their workshops and kilns smoked here, turning out cups, jugs and storage jars, and the black and red painted vases that now sit in museums all over the world. The painters often signed their work, and some added little notes praising the good looks of someone they admired.

Just outside the old city gate, along the roads leading out of town, was where Athenians buried their families, and the potters' quarter grew up alongside the cemetery. Many of the gravestones show quiet farewells: two people clasping hands, a mother with her child, a woman with her jewellery box. The stone of Hegeso, which you can see at the National Archaeological Museum, was found here. Today it's a peaceful place of grass and old stones, with wildflowers in spring, frogs in the stream, and the odd tortoise making its way between the graves.

Metaxourgeio today is a mixed and changing neighbourhood. You'll find old neoclassical houses, some crumbling and some beautifully restored, alongside small theatres, galleries, street art and family-run tavernas. Older residents and newcomers live side by side, and artists have been drawn here by lower rents and big old rooms.

So as the bus passes, you're between two old kinds of making, the silk that women spun here a century and a half ago, and the clay that potters shaped just down the road more than two and a half thousand years ago.

Monastiraki takes its name from the little monastery church that still sits in its square, and the area has been a marketplace for a very long time. In the lanes around Avyssinias Square, the flea market sells everything from copper pots and old records to lace, furniture and army surplus, and on Sundays it spills out into the streets. Near the square stand the walls of Hadrian's Library, built by the emperor in the second century as a quiet place of books and gardens around a long pool.

Beyond the railway line is the Ancient Agora, the heart of the old city, its marketplace and its meeting place, where people came to shop, to settle disputes and to gossip. Near one corner, archaeologists found a small house and workshop whose floor was scattered with iron hobnails, the kind used in the soles of sandals, along with the base of a cup scratched with the name Simon. Ancient writers tell of Simon the shoemaker, at whose workshop Socrates would sit and talk with young men who weren't yet old enough to take part in the business of the Agora. It's a lovely thought, that some of the first philosophy may have been done to the tap of a cobbler's hammer.

On the low hill above the Agora stands the Temple of Hephaestus, the god of metalworkers and craftsmen, one of the best-preserved Greek temples anywhere, with bronze-smiths and potters once working all around it. In the nineteen thirties, an American archaeologist named Dorothy Burr Thompson found rows of pits cut into the rock around the temple, with bits of flowerpots in them. They showed that more than two thousand years ago there had been a garden here. In nineteen fifty-six the garden was replanted, and today shrubs and trees grow around the temple much as they once did. The Agora's cats approve, and doze in its shade.

In the evening, walk along Apostolou Pavlou, the pedestrian street that curves from Thissio along the foot of the hills. This is where Athenians take the volta, the slow evening stroll: grandparents arm in arm, couples, children on scooters, friends stopping for a drink at a café table while the Acropolis turns gold above them and then lights up.

In summer, the open-air Cine Thission, here since nineteen thirty-five, shows films under the stars, with the floodlit Parthenon just behind the screen. Bring a cardigan; even in Athens, the evenings can surprise you.

This is the Central Market, the Varvakeios, and it's been the city's kitchen since it opened in the eighteen eighties. The covered halls hold the meat and fish markets, where stallholders call out their prices over beds of ice. Across Athinas Street, the fruit and vegetable sellers pile up whatever is in season, figs and grapes at the end of summer, oranges and greens in winter. In the lanes around, small shops sell olives, cheese, dried herbs, beans and spices by weight, much as they have for generations. Early in the morning, the restaurant cooks and the grandmothers arrive first, and both know exactly what they want.

In the hard years after twenty ten, when the debt crisis cut wages and pensions and, at its worst, left more than a quarter of working people without a job, the market was one of the places where you could see the city coping. Shoppers bought a little less and chose more carefully. Many families moved back in together, with a grandparent's pension stretched to feed three generations. In neighbourhoods all over Athens, volunteers set up shared kitchens, food banks and free clinics. Greece left its last bailout programme in twenty eighteen, and life is steadier now, but many families still remember those years every time they write a shopping list.

Opposite is Kotzia Square, with City Hall along one side. For half a century, the square's centrepiece was the grand Municipal Theatre, designed by Ernst Ziller and opened in eighteen eighty-eight. In nineteen twenty-two, refugee families from Asia Minor made their homes in its boxes, a household in each little room. The theatre was pulled down around the start of the Second World War.

When an underground car park was planned in the nineteen eighties, archaeologists dug first, and found an ancient road with the ruts of cart wheels still in its surface, family tombs along its edges, and Roman potters' workshops with their kilns. If you're hopping off to eat, here's how Athens does it, and a few places that have been doing it for a long time. Hours change, so it's always worth checking the door.

The city's fast food is souvlaki. Ask for a pitta souvlaki and you get grilled meat, usually pork, wrapped in warm flatbread with tomato, onion and tzatziki, for a few euros, eaten standing up. Gyros, sliced from the turning stack, comes the same way. The classic place to try it is around Monastiraki Square, at the bottom of Mitropoleos Street. Bairaktaris, on the square itself, has been a taverna since eighteen seventy-nine, and Thanasis, a few doors away, is famous for its kebab, minced meat grilled on a skewer and served with flatbread, onion and parsley. Near Syntagma, Kostas, a tiny counter on Pentelis Street, traces its souvlaki back to nineteen fifty.

Breakfast is often eaten on the move. Look for the carts selling koulouri, rings of bread crusted with sesame, which are cheap and best early. Bakeries sell bougatsa, custard or cheese wrapped in filo, served warm with icing sugar and cinnamon. For pies, Ariston on Voulis Street, near Syntagma, has been baking since nineteen ten, and its cheese pie in buttery pastry has fed generations of politicians.

For something sweet, Krinos on Aiolou Street, near the market, has been frying loukoumades since nineteen twenty-three: little puffs of dough soaked in honey and dusted with cinnamon, in a room with mosaic floors and marble tables that hasn't changed much since. Near Omonia, on Marikas Kotopouli Street, the old dairy bar Stani has been serving thick sheep's milk yogurt with honey since nineteen thirty-one.

Then there are the market tavernas, the best lunch on this route if you don't mind paper tablecloths. Inside the Central Market, Epirus serves pots of the day's stews and soups, including patsas, the tripe soup that market workers swear by and that Athenians swear cures a hangover. Just behind the market on Sokratous Street is Diporto, down a few steps in a basement. There's no real menu, the wine comes from the barrel, and you eat whatever is cooking that day: chickpeas, a salad, fried fish. It's been there for well over a century, and the poet Kostas Varnalis put it in a poem.

To drink, try retsina, white wine flavoured with pine resin, which goes surprisingly well with fried fish, or an ouzo with a small plate of meze; it turns cloudy when you add water. And as everywhere in Greece, nobody brings the bill until you ask for it. Kali orexi. Enjoy your meal.
